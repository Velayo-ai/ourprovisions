What changes, and what it costs

Today’s problem isn’t the rail — it’s that a phone-width column in a 1,400px window reads as unfinished rather than intentional. Both options fix that the same way: stop apologising for the shape and frame it.

Both options retire the rail

This is the consequence worth deciding on. Once the content sits in a framed column, a navigation rail outside that frame is a second nav for the same four doors. The helm belongs inside. That collapses nav.js to one renderer, deletes <Rail /> and the 700px media query, and removes the “which nav am I looking at” question at every width. Less code than today, not more.

A versus B

  • A reads as a product that knows what it is. The shadow and the radius do all the work; nothing is pretending. It survives being looked at every day, and it dates well because there’s nothing to date.
  • B is more charming for exactly one audience — someone you are showing it to — and it says “phone app” in half a second without a word. The risk is that a device chassis is the visual language of demos and emulators, so a tester opening it on their laptop may read it as a prototype rather than the real thing.

Recommendation: A. The margins are the actual opportunity — on the phone the household photo is a cropped banner, and on desktop it can be the whole room. That’s the asset that makes OurProvisions feel like someone’s home, and desktop is the one place there’s space to let it breathe. A device bezel would cover that with plastic.

If you want B for demos specifically, it’s a body class rather than a layout — same column, different wrapper. Worth doing only if you find yourself wanting it twice.

Cost

A max-width, a radius, a shadow, a full-bleed background, and deleting the rail branch. One commit, and it makes the codebase smaller.
